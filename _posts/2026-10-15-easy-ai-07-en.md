<h1>Why Can an AI Answer<br />Be Wrong?</h1>

<blockquote>AI can write natural-sounding sentences, but a confident tone does not automatically make the facts correct.<br />This lesson explains hallucinations and four simple ways to check an answer before relying on it.<br /></blockquote>

<figure><img src="{{ '/assets/images/easy-ai-07-checking-answer.svg' | relative_url }}" alt="A person checks an AI-generated answer against an original source document"></figure>

<h2>A hallucination is a plausible but false answer. <a class="source-badge" href="https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8313428">OpenAI guidance</a></h2>

<p><strong>Hallucination</strong> is the term used when an AI presents information that is not true as if it were true.<br />It may invent or distort dates, people, book titles, quotations, or source links, and it may choose one confident answer when a question is unclear.<br /></p>

<p>“Hallucination” does not mean that an AI is seeing something like a person does.<br />It is a shorthand for a natural-sounding answer whose content does not match the facts.<br /></p>

<h2>Why might AI answer even when it does not know?</h2>

<p>A language model generates an answer by using patterns in the text that comes before it.<br />Producing a smooth sentence and checking whether its facts are true are not the same ability.<br /></p>

<p>OpenAI’s research explanation says that language models can be rewarded for guessing rather than acknowledging uncertainty when they are trained and evaluated.<br />That can contribute to confident wrong answers when a model does not have a reliable answer.<br /><a href="https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/">Read OpenAI’s research explanation</a><br /></p>

<blockquote>A natural-sounding answer is not the same as a verified fact.</blockquote>

<h2>Check these answers especially carefully.</h2>

<ul>
<li>Today’s prices, stock, opening hours, or other frequently changing details.</li>
<li>Reservations, schedules, and supported regions that depend on current status.</li>
<li>Medical, legal, financial, or safety information where an error could cause harm.</li>
<li>Quotations, papers, books, laws, or statistics that require an original source.</li>
</ul>

<p>An AI may show a source link, but that is not the end of the check.<br />You still need to see whether the link opens, whether the original text supports the claim, and whether it applies to your situation.<br /></p>

<h2>Use these four steps to check an answer.</h2>

<p><strong>First, choose the claims that need checking.</strong><br />Instead of investigating every sentence equally, start with dates, numbers, names, quotations, and other details that would matter if wrong.<br /></p>

<p><strong>Second, find the original source.</strong><br />Prefer the official agency, product documentation, or original paper that first published the information.<br /></p>

<p><strong>Third, compare the source with the answer.</strong><br />Check whether the original has a narrower scope, an exception, a condition, or a different date that the AI left out.<br /></p>

<p><strong>Fourth, pause if you cannot verify it.</strong><br />If you cannot find the original source or reliable sources disagree, do not use the answer immediately for an important decision.<br /></p>

<h2>Asking AI to check itself can help, but human review still matters.</h2>

<p>You can ask an AI to say when it is unsure, attach a source to each claim, or mark statements that need verification.<br />Those requests can help you find places to review.<br /></p>

<p>They do not remove the possibility of error.<br />You still need to compare the suggested sources with the original documents, and you should not hand an important decision over to the AI.<br /></p>

<h2>One-line takeaway.</h2>

<p><strong>A confident AI tone is not fact-checking, so compare important dates, numbers, and conditions with the original source yourself.</strong><br /></p>
