<h1>Common Words You Meet<br />When Using AI</h1>
<blockquote>AI service descriptions can introduce several English words at once.<br />Here is a short guide to recognizing the terms on screen and connecting them with simple examples.</blockquote>

<h2>You do not need to memorize these words.</h2>
<p>Using AI well is less about knowing many technical words and more about clearly describing what you need and checking important answers.<br />It is enough to recognize the words below when they appear in a service description or settings screen.<br /></p>

<h2>AI and generative AI.</h2>
<p><strong>AI (Artificial Intelligence)</strong> is a broad term for technology that helps computers perform tasks that once needed human judgment.<br />A phone keyboard suggesting the next word, a photo app grouping pictures of people or pets, and a map app recommending a faster route are everyday examples of AI.<br /></p>
<p><strong>Generative AI</strong> is AI that creates new outputs such as text, images, sound, or code from a request.<br />Pasting meeting notes into ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude and asking, “Summarize the key points in three lines,” or asking an image tool for a first draft of an event poster are examples of using generative AI.<br />ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude are familiar services that use generative AI; they are not the whole category.<br /><a href="https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/glossary/generative?hl=en">Read Google’s official generative AI glossary</a><br /></p>

<h2>Models and services.</h2>
<p>A <strong>model</strong> is the underlying technology an AI uses to produce an answer or result.<br />Names you may see for models that handle writing and conversation include GPT, Gemini, Claude, Llama, Mistral, Qwen, and DeepSeek.<br />For example, the ChatGPT service uses GPT-family models, while the Gemini service uses Gemini-family models.<br />When you enter the same question into different AI services and get different wording or organization, the models and settings behind those services may differ.<br />Like a car engine that affects how the car moves even when you do not see it, a model affects the character of a result behind the screen.<br /></p>
<p>A <strong>service</strong> is the website or app interface and features that make a model usable in practice.<br />ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude are conversational services; Microsoft Copilot can help with document and email work; Perplexity is often used to look for sources.<br />Names such as Gemini and Claude can be used for both a service and a model family, so it is natural if the meaning is not immediately clear on screen.<br />Think of a model as a recipe and a service as a restaurant where you can order and receive food.<br />One service can use several models, and one model can be used in more than one service.<br /></p>

<h2>An LLM is a model that works with writing and conversation.</h2>
<p>An <strong>LLM (Large Language Model)</strong> is a type of model used to read human language, continue writing, and answer questions.<br />The generative AI behind tasks such as polishing an email, summarizing a long text, translating a sentence, or organizing ideas in a conversation often uses an LLM.<br />GPT, Gemini, Claude, Llama, Mistral, Qwen, and DeepSeek are representative examples of LLMs or LLM families; the list is not complete.<br /></p>
<p>Not every AI is an LLM.<br />An AI that sorts people in a photo app or recommends a route in a map app may not center on language conversation.<br />By contrast, when you enter a prompt into a conversational AI, an LLM processes the writing in tokens and can refer to earlier conversation inside its context window to make an answer.<br />That is why it can write natural language without guaranteeing that every statement is true; important answers still need checking.<br /><a href="https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/llm/transformers">Read Google’s official LLM guide</a><br /></p>

<h2>Prompts and context.</h2>
<p>A <strong>prompt</strong> is the question, instruction, or material you give an AI.<br />If you paste an email draft and write, “Make this refusal sound polite,” that whole request is a prompt.<br />Adding the format you want—“in three sentences,” “as a table,” or “with examples a child can understand”—can bring the answer closer to what you need.<br /></p>
<p><strong>Context</strong> is the flow of earlier conversation and current input that an AI can refer to while making an answer.<br />In a conversation about dinner, if you say, “Remove spicy food and make it something we can cook in 30 minutes,” the people, ingredients, and time you discussed earlier are the context.<br />The same sentence in a new chat may get a different answer because the earlier conversation is not there.<br /></p>

<h2>Tokens and a context window.</h2>
<p>A <strong>token</strong> is a small unit an AI uses to process text.<br />A token is not exactly the same as a character or word, but it often comes up when explaining whether a system can handle a long report, contract, or meeting record at once.<br />For example, if a short note can be pasted directly but a several-hundred-page document must be divided, that may relate to the amount of text the system can process in tokens.<br /></p>
<p>A <strong>context window</strong> is the range of information an AI can consider together in one conversation.<br />If an AI loses a condition agreed at the beginning of a customer-inquiry summary you have continued over several days, that old detail may no longer be fully available within the current context window.<br />It is safer to paste important requirements, tables, and sources again when needed.<br /></p>

<h2>A hallucination is a plausible but false answer.</h2>
<p>A <strong>hallucination</strong> happens when an AI creates information that is not true but sounds convincing.<br />For example, when asked for this year’s date for a local festival or the author of a book that does not exist, it may give a date, name, and link that look real.<br />It can happen when a system guesses instead of saying that it does not know something.<br /></p>
<p>For dates, numbers, quotations, and sources—and for important topics such as health, law, or finance—do not treat an AI answer as the final check.<br />Verify it in an original source or with an official organization.<br />It can also help to ask AI to say when it is unsure or to include sources.<br /><a href="https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/">Read OpenAI’s explanation of hallucinations</a><br /></p>

<h2>These are enough to remember at first.</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>AI (Artificial Intelligence):</strong> A broad term for technology that helps with tasks people do</li>
<li><strong>Generative AI:</strong> AI that creates new text, images, code, and other outputs</li>
<li><strong>Model:</strong> The underlying technology that produces an AI result</li>
<li><strong>LLM (Large Language Model):</strong> A type of model that works with writing and conversation</li>
<li><strong>Service:</strong> A website or app that makes a model usable</li>
<li><strong>Prompt:</strong> The question or instruction you enter</li>
<li><strong>Context:</strong> The conversation flow an AI can refer to</li>
<li><strong>Token:</strong> A small unit an AI uses to process text</li>
<li><strong>Context window:</strong> The range of information an AI can consider together</li>
<li><strong>Hallucination:</strong> A plausible answer that may not be true</li>
</ul>

<h2>In one sentence.</h2>
<p class="summary"><strong>You do not need to memorize every AI term.<br />When you see these English words on screen, connect a model with underlying technology, a prompt with your request, context with the flow of conversation, and a hallucination with a convincing error that needs checking.</strong><br /></p>
<p>In the next lesson, we will look more simply at how to write a prompt that gets closer to the answer you want.<br /></p>
