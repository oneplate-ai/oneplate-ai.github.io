<p class="post-kicker">Daily AI Bite</p>
<h1>September 14, 2026</h1>
<blockquote>This issue looks at three ways AI is being used to test software, create new forms of storytelling, and make sure development is paced so that safeguards can keep up.<br />As AI produces more results, evidence of how those results were checked and human judgment matter just as much.<br /></blockquote>

<h2>1. OpenAI shared a case study of software testing that shows the results. <a class="source-badge" href="https://openai.com/index/cognition-devin-testing-with-astra">OpenAI announcement</a></h2>
<p class="multi-sentence">On September 11, OpenAI shared a case study in which Cognition’s Devin uses GPT-6 Astra to test software it has created and show evidence that it works.<br />Devin is a tool that performs software tasks autonomously; in this example, it returns a recording of an iPhone game running in a simulator plus a report of passed checks and areas that remain untested.<br /></p>
<p class="multi-sentence">The approach moves beyond saying that an AI wrote code by providing evidence from an actual run.<br />The case study and capability descriptions come from OpenAI and Cognition, so development teams still need to judge whether the recording and report cover enough of the risk.<br /></p>
<ul><li><strong>Who it may help:</strong> Development teams using AI coding tools or automating software tests.</li><li><strong>Current status:</strong> This was presented as a Cognition case study; it does not mean every developer can access the same capability under the same conditions.<br /></li><li><strong>Why it matters:</strong> Automated coding work also needs results that can be reproduced and checked.<br /></li><li><strong>What to watch:</strong> A recording and report may cover only part of testing, so people still need to review scope and risk.<br /></li></ul>

<h2>2. Google introduced XR storytelling projects built with Gemini. <a class="source-badge" href="https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/xr-ar/three-google-supported-projects-premiere-during-the-83rd-venice-international-film-festival/">Google announcement</a></h2>
<p class="multi-sentence">On September 11, Google introduced three extended-reality (XR) projects premiering at the 83rd Venice International Film Festival.<br />XR overlays digital visuals or interaction onto a real-world setting, and these projects use conversational AI powered by Gemini and Vertex AI.<br /></p>
<p class="multi-sentence">For example, “NEVATARS” is described as a mixed-reality animated short with Gemini-powered conversations and interactive moments, while “Galápagos: The Last Eden” lets viewers interact with a digital Charles Darwin in an immersive nature experience.<br />Google presents the projects as experiments in keeping human creativity at the center while technology expands the form of a story.<br /></p>
<ul><li><strong>Who it may help:</strong> Creators and audiences experimenting with new formats for film, games, and exhibitions.</li><li><strong>Current status:</strong> These projects were announced for the September 11 festival program; access and viewing conditions may differ by project.<br /></li><li><strong>Why it matters:</strong> It shows AI becoming part of an interactive story rather than only generating text or images.<br /></li><li><strong>What to watch:</strong> The project descriptions and technical effects are Google’s account; actual quality can depend on the device and venue.<br /></li></ul>

<h2>3. Anthropic’s CEO proposed slowing the pace of AI capability growth. <a class="source-badge" href="https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier">Dario Amodei’s essay</a></h2>
<p class="multi-sentence">On September 12, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei proposed slowing the rate at which AI models gain capabilities so that safety research and risk prevention can have time to keep up.<br />He described this “pacing” not as stopping development or training, but as making sure safety, alignment, and security checks happen before models become more capable.<br /></p>
<p class="multi-sentence">As a first step, he proposed giving external evaluators continuing access inside companies to check whether safety commitments are followed and to report incidents.<br />The proposal argues for competition around verifiable safety standards rather than speed alone.<br /></p>
<p class="multi-sentence">After the essay, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman agreed with the direction and said OpenAI would also use independent evaluators with employee-like access.<br />Elon Musk, CEO of xAI, also offered brief support, writing that “Dario is right.”<br /></p>
<ul><li><strong>Who it may affect:</strong> Companies building frontier AI models, along with governments, businesses, and development teams that adopt them.</li><li><strong>Current status:</strong> This is Anthropic’s public proposal, followed by public reactions from the CEOs of OpenAI and xAI; it is not an industry-wide agreement or binding rule.<br /></li><li><strong>Why it matters:</strong> CEOs of competing AI companies are publicly discussing a pace that allows safety research, outside evaluation, and public deliberation to keep up.<br /></li><li><strong>What to watch:</strong> Public support and follow-up intentions are not the same as a detailed timetable, verification scope, or legal obligation, so actual implementation still needs to be checked.<br /></li></ul>

<h2>Today in one sentence.</h2>
<p class="summary">As AI enters work and creative production, showing how a result was checked is becoming as important as the result itself.<br /></p>
